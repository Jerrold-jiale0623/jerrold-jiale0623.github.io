The car is aimed at Nürburgring lap attempts. I set its downforce and drag targets and designed the next-generation package in CAD: splitter, side skirts, swan-neck wing mounts and an adjustable wing-angle mechanism.

The brief

  • A 330 km/h-class electric sedan, developed from the production Exlantix ES, aimed at Nürburgring lap attempts.
  • On an EV every drag count costs range, so downforce has to be earned efficiently.
  • Targets framed against Porsche Taycan Turbo GT (Manthey Kit) Nürburgring data.

I shaped a long-tail GT in CFD, printed a scale model and tested it on an aerodynamic balance. The tunnel read lower drag than CFD, and downforce where CFD had predicted lift.

The brief

  • A GT cruises at 100–150 km/h, where aerodynamic drag is over 80% of running resistance.
  • It needs a very low drag coefficient for range and balanced downforce for stability, with no wings breaking the silhouette.

From open loop to a robot that holds itself up

200 Hz
Balance loop, one pass every 5 ms
α 0.95
Complementary filter, gyro-weighted
5 / 5
Open-ended challenges attempted

An open-ended module with no handholding: five challenges of rising difficulty on a two-wheeled "Lab-in-a-Bag" robot. We went from steering motors by tilting the board, to holding wheel speed under load with PID, to choreography synced to live music, and finally to the classic inverted-pendulum problem: keep the robot upright by driving its wheels under it.

Five steps up

  1. 01
    Beat-reactive LEDsLoudness and beat light the strip
  2. 02
    Tilt to drivePitch drives motor A, roll drives motor B
  3. 03
    PID speed holdEncoder feedback, 100 ms window
  4. 04
    Dance to the beatASCII choreography, live music
  5. 05
    Self-balancingPitch PID at 200 Hz

Each step built on the last: the IMU pitch from step 2 and the PID loop from step 3 carried straight into step 5.

Inside the loops

  • Speed PID: encoder pulses counted by interrupts and read every 100 ms; integral clamped at ±500 against wind-up; a 3° dead band stops the wheels twitching at rest. Tested by loading the wheels by hand.
  • Beat detection: microphone sampled at 8 kHz in 160-sample windows; a beat is an energy spike 1.45× the moving average, rising, above a calibrated noise floor, and at least 320 ms after the last.
  • Balance: accelerometer pitch is noisy, gyro rate drifts, so a complementary filter fuses both. The D-term uses the gyro rate directly instead of differentiating a noisy angle.
  • Timing: the OLED is kept out of the 200 Hz loop because a screen update is too slow to fit in the 5 ms budget.

Tuning notes

  • Kp −4too little restoring torque, falls
  • Kp −8over-corrects, fast oscillation
  • Kp −4.5first pass inside the window

Gains were tuned live with the on-board potentiometer. Sweeping Kp alone first narrowed the window to −4 … −8 before I and D were added.

Set point −1.6°, not 0°, to offset the centre of mass. A sign-check mode printed accelerometer pitch next to gyro rate, so a polarity mismatch between sensor and motors could be caught before tuning.

Smart planters turn a living thing into numbers on an app. Hachi-Pot gives the plant a real mechanical face instead: content in the sun, happy when touched, thirsty when dry, asleep at night. I led the CAD, structure and mechanism; a teammate led the programming.

Four moods, read from three senses

  • ContentBright light
  • HappyTouched
  • ThirstyDry soil
  • AsleepDark

Eight layers, top to base

  1. 01Upper inner bracketPlant cup
  2. 02Upper mounting plateServo mount
  3. 03Main upper shellBody
  4. 04ServoLifts the plant
  5. 05Outer shell, face windowStatic frame
  6. 06Stepper + expression wheel6 positions
  7. 07Lower support plateElectronics
  8. 08Base plateDrive and wheels

A cup-in-cup layout: the servo lifts the plant while the stepper turns the middle layer to pick a face. The static outer shell frames both, so the moving parts read as one character. Amber marks the moving layers.

Drive base and electronics

An ESP32 reads the light and soil-moisture sensors and drives the servo and stepper; an L298N runs the wheel motors.

The fixAt the first low-fidelity prototype nothing fitted: no room for components, no way to mount the servo, no route for the wiring.

The callMy teammate and I agreed that changing the hardware would cost the least time, rather than bending the electronics or code around a flawed enclosure.

Rev BI redrew the layers with tighter tolerances so every part had a defined place. It assembled cleanly and the demo went well.

Stackin' Beats, music you build with your hands

DS2401A+
1-Wire ID chip in each brick
7.5 / 10
Parent and specialist rating

A music-making kit, pitched as a concept for LEGO Education, that builds fine motor skills for children with autism. Children press bricks into a row to compose a melody, then play it with a slider. As CTO I owned the circuit, the mechanism, the iterations and the working prototype.

Who it is for

~80%of children with autism find fine motor tasks challenging. Traditional instruments demand precise finger placement, which shuts many of them out.

What parents and an occupational therapist told us
  • Familiar, LEGO-like bricks lower the barrier to starting.
  • Everything must be reachable from a seated position, with no fallen parts to chase.
  • Classroom use means many hands a day, so durability is a user need.
  • A child should make a melody alone after one demonstration.

Audio, power and cost

  • Speaker: CMS-4021-34SP-X5, 26 g and up to 88 dB, loud enough for a classroom.
  • Clean sound: playing several notes at once added noise. A low-pass filter cleaned it up; an LQ-AMP10W amplifier won back the lost volume and adds a volume dial.
  • Headphone jack: many children in our user group use headphones to regulate their emotions, so they can choose how they listen.
  • Power bank: the system needs about 21.6 Ah, which rules out coin cells and AAs. A rechargeable bank keeps it portable with no trailing cable.

Part selection

Each part was chosen against a real alternative on three tests: does it work with the rest of the system, does it meet the technical requirement, and what does it cost. Chosen part in white, the option we dropped struck through.

Mechanism
Snail camvsEgg cam

A snail cam gives a slow rise then a sharp drop, so the eight followers draw a much clearer wave without adding parts.

Mechanism

The camshaft only needs to spin continuously. A NEMA 11 stepper would draw far more power for a light rotary load.

Tolerances and build

0.1 mmpress-fit tolerance on the 3 × 3 cm bricks, so children with fine-motor difficulties can stack and pull them apart without a fight.

  • Bricks printed on FDM with a 0.2 mm nozzle; the CAD allows for shrinkage, perimeter expansion and surface roughness.
  • Each brick is a two-part housing around its chip, closed by snap-fits with a locating lip.
  • Casing laser-cut in plywood, so one panel could be swapped after each iteration instead of reprinting the whole box.
  • Printed snap-fit followers self-align with the camshaft. The base board and cam sub-assembly went together in 5 min 47 s.

Testing with users

  • A/B test: a slanted front and external brick storage kept children upright and focused on the music, not the hardware.
  • Great Exhibition Road Festival, June 2026: children jumped straight in. Giving less information at a time held their attention longer.
  • Parents: worried most about durability, which pushed the snap-fit and housing design.
  • Growth: separate left- and right-hand platforms let fine-motor and musical skills progress visibly over time.

Designed for production

  • Cams injection-moulded in acetal (POM): strong, low-friction and hard-wearing against the followers.
  • An indexed fixture sets each cam 45° from the last, avoiding a costly keyed shaft.
  • Four brass M2 inserts hold a removable base, so parts can be repaired rather than replaced.

A running blade, tuned out of resonance

First mode, target above 50 Hz
16.6

A carbon-fibre prosthetic blade had to survive 2100 N vertical and 500 N friction loads while keeping its first natural frequency above running cadence. It took three geometries to meet every requirement.

Requirements against results

CheckRequiredFinal
Static safety factor> 316.6
First natural frequency> 50 Hz51.7 Hz
Fatigue life10⁶ cycles10⁸ cycles
Fatigue safety factor–2.98
  • Loads 2100 N vertical and 500 N friction at the toe; fixed support at the socket face.
  • Material Woven epoxy carbon prepreg, 230 GPa, 805 MPa tensile strength.

Mesh convergence

Peak principal stress moved less than 2% from 5 mm to 1 mm. Frequency was more sensitive: coarse linear elements over-stiffen thin bending parts (shear locking), so the 51.7 Hz result uses a refined mesh with quadratic elements.

Engineering choices

  • Max principal stress, not von Mises. CFRP is brittle and fails in tension, so the Rankine criterion governs the safety factor.
  • Aluminium for fatigue. The brief called for a ductile alloy with a validated S-N curve, a zero-based load and Gerber mean-stress correction.
  • Taper, not a step. Design 2 showed that an abrupt section change makes a stress singularity; a continuous taper spreads the load path.
Limits and next steps

Running loads were modelled as equivalent static loads and the socket as a rigid fixed support. Explicit dynamics for heel strike and a spring-foundation socket would be the next steps.
